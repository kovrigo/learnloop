# CHAPTER 1 The Cyclops
The most famous trip home in history|took about ten years.
He spent eight of them as a houseguest:|seven years with the nymph Calypso,|one with the witch Circe.
And when he finally got home,|a hundred and eight houseguests|were eating his food.

## gap 90
This is Homer's Odyssey,|a poem about 2,700 years old.
Troy has just fallen after ten years of war.
Odysseus, king of the island of Ithaca,|sets sail for home with twelve ships.

Waiting at home are his wife, Penelope,|and a son who was a baby when he left.
It should be a quick trip.
It is not.

The monsters come from one dinner party,|where Odysseus tells the story himself.
So this is his version.

Early on, his men walk into the cave|of a giant Cyclops called Polyphemus.
He traps them inside|and eats six of the crew, two at a time.
Odysseus tells the giant his name is Nobody.
That night, while the giant sleeps,|they blind him with a stake|hardened in the fire.

He screams for help,|and his neighbors ask who is hurting him.
Nobody, he shouts.
So they shrug and go home.
At dawn, the men escape|under the bellies of his rams.

A perfect escape, until Odysseus ruins it.
From his ship, he shouts his real name,|his father's name,|and his home island.
History's first case of sharing your location|with the wrong person.

The Cyclops is a son of Poseidon,|god of the sea.
He prays to his father:|let Odysseus get home late, alone,|on someone else's ship.
Poseidon won't kill Odysseus,|but he'll keep him away from home.
And between here and Ithaca,|there is a lot of sea.

# CHAPTER 2 Stops at Sea
From here on, every stop goes wrong.
The keeper of the winds|gives him a bag|with the storm winds tied inside.
For nine days they sail straight home,|and with Ithaca in sight, he falls asleep.

His crew thinks the bag is full of gold.
They open it,|and the winds blow them all the way back.
Recalculating.

Next, a tribe of giants|smashes every ship but his.
Twelve ships left Troy.
Now there is one.

On the next island,|Circe turns his men into pigs.
Pig bodies, human minds.
Hermes gives Odysseus a herb|that blocks her magic.
It works.
He stays a year anyway.

Then come the Sirens.
In Homer there are just two,|and what they promise is knowledge.
Odysseus plugs his crew's ears with wax|and has himself tied to the mast.
Noise-cancelling for the crew,|a live concert for the captain.

Next, a strait with two bad options.
Scylla, a monster with six heads,|or Charybdis, a whirlpool|that swallows the sea three times a day.
Scylla takes six of his men.

On the island of the sun god,|the starving crew eats his sacred cattle.
Zeus sinks the ship with a thunderbolt,|and only Odysseus survives.

He washes up on Calypso's island.
She keeps him for seven years,|and offers to make him immortal.
He says no.
He wants to go home,|where his wife has a problem of her own.

# CHAPTER 3 Home in Ithaca
So Odysseus is stuck on one island.
Penelope is stuck on another.
A hundred and eight men want to marry her,|and they won't leave until she picks one.

Meanwhile, they party in the palace every day,|on his cattle, his sheep and his wine.
They eat everything in sight,|and they'd still give the place one star.

Penelope stalls.
She'll choose a husband, she says,|once she finishes weaving a shroud|for Odysseus's father.
She weaves it by day|and secretly unpicks it by night.
For three years.

In the fourth year, a maid gives her away.
Meanwhile, their son, the baby from the start,|is grown up.
He sails off to find news of his father,|guided by an old friend called Mentor.

Mentor is really Athena in disguise,|and that's where our word mentor comes from.
The suitors plan to ambush him on his way home.
Now Penelope is out of excuses,|the suitors are out of patience,|and Odysseus is out of ships.

# CHAPTER 4 The Return
The gods finally order Calypso to let him go.
A kingdom of sailors carries him|the rest of the way, fast asleep.

Twenty years after he left,|Odysseus is home.
Late, alone, on someone else's ship.
Exactly what the Cyclops asked for.
You have arrived.

Athena disguises him as an old beggar,|so he can scout his own house.
His old dog, Argos, knows him at once,|wags his tail one last time, and dies.
His old nurse knows him by a scar on his leg.

Then Penelope sets a test.
Whoever strings Odysseus's old bow|and shoots an arrow through twelve axes|will be her husband.
The suitors try and fail.
The beggar strings it as easily as a lyre,|and shoots through all twelve.
Then the rags come off,|and the suitors' very long party is over.

Penelope still isn't sure,|so she asks a servant to move their bed.
Odysseus is outraged: nobody can move that bed.
One of its posts is an olive tree,|still rooted in the ground.
Only the real Odysseus would know that.
She has out-tricked the master trickster.

When the suitors' families come for revenge,|Athena makes both sides swear peace.
The poem ends there.
The story doesn't.

# CHAPTER 5 Odyssey Today
It moved into our language.
A long, hard journey is an odyssey,|a meaning English has used since the late 1800s.
Stuck between two dangers?|You're between Scylla and Charybdis.

In 2017, Emily Wilson published|the first English translation of the poem|by a woman.
Her first line: Tell me about a complicated man.
The Greek word means, roughly,|a man of many turns.

This summer, Christopher Nolan's film|took in about 1.77 billion dollars.
And by mid-July, before it even opened,|US print sales of the poem|were up 76 percent year to date.
Just know the film skips the Nobody trick,|and on screen, Athena is the only god.

Quick game: real or fake?
One: the Trojan Horse is in the Odyssey.
Two: Odysseus's dog recognizes him|after twenty years.
Three: Homer says the Cyclops has one eye|in the middle of his forehead.

## gap 60
The fake is number three.
Homer only mentions his eye, singular,|and never says where it is.
The forehead comes from Hesiod,|describing different Cyclopes.
The horse is real.
It's in the Odyssey three times,|and never in the Iliad.

So why did a trip home take ten years?
One boast, one angry sea god,|and some very long visits.
Next time you post where you are,|think of Odysseus.

But why was he at Troy in the first place?
